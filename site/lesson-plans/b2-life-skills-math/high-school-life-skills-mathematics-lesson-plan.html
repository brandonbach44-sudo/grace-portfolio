Making Purchases & Calculating Change (sample lesson)

Money & BankingLife Skills Math HS

PA Standards

  • Pennsylvania Academic Standards for Mathematics – Numbers and Operations
  • Students will apply addition and subtraction skills to solve mathematical problems involving quantities and real-world situations.
  • Standards for Mathematical Practice:
  • MP1: Make sense of problems and persevere in solving them.
  • MP2: Reason abstractly and quantitatively.
  • MP4: Model with mathematics.
  • MP5: Use appropriate tools strategically.
  • MP6: Attend to precision.
  • Functional/Personal Finance Connection:
  • Students will apply mathematical skills to a functional spending situation by identifying prices, determining the total cost of purchases, and calculating change.

Essential Questions

  • How can I use math to make a purchase and determine how much change I should receive?
  •  How much does the item cost?
  •  How much money do I need to pay?
  •  Do I need to add the prices together?
  •  How can I find my change?
  •  How can I check that my answer is correct?

Objectives

  • Student Objective:
  • Given a simulated shopping situation, price tags, play money, and individualized supports, students will use money to make a purchase and calculate the total cost and/or amount of change appropriate to their instructional level with at least 80% accuracy.
  • Students will be able to:
  • Identify the value of common coins and bills.
  • Identify the price of an item.
  • Add the cost of two or more items when appropriate.
  • Select an appropriate amount of money to use for a purchase.
  • Calculate the amount of change received.
  • Use a calculator or other mathematical tool appropriately when provided as an accommodation.
  • Demonstrate understanding through written responses, verbal responses, selecting an answer, or manipulating money.

Assessment

  • Students will demonstrate their understanding through teacher questioning, manipulation of play money, guided practice, individualized independent practice, and a final shopping problem.
  • Formative Checks:
  • Identification of coins and bills during the warm-up
  • Teacher questioning
  • Observation during teacher modeling
  • Responses during guided practice
  • Student use of play money
  • Teacher observation checklist
  • Individualized practice problems
  • Teacher feedback and prompting
  • Summative/End-of-Lesson Check:
  • Students will complete one individualized shopping problem during the final five minutes of class.
  • Examples may include:
  • Beginning level:
  • Identify the correct money for an item costing $1.
  • Developing level:
  • An item costs $3. You pay with $5. How much change do you receive?
  • More independent level:
  • A notebook costs $3.50 and a folder costs $2.50. What is the total? If you pay with $10, how much change do you receive?
  • Students will demonstrate mastery by correctly completing at least 4 out of 5 assigned problems (80%) when completing the larger individualized practice assessment. The difficulty of the problems will be adjusted to each student's instructional level.

Differentiation

  • Instruction and assessment will be differentiated according to students' instructional levels, IEP goals, accommodations, and individual needs.
  • Students requiring greater support:
  • Identify individual coins and bills.
  • Match money to prices.
  • Use whole-dollar amounts.
  • Use play money.
  • Use a visual step-by-step sequence.
  • Use a number line.
  • Receive repeated or simplified directions.
  • Work with one item at a time.
  • Receive teacher prompting.
  • Students working at an intermediate level:
  • Calculate the cost of two items.
  • Determine change from $5 or $10.
  • Use visual supports as needed.
  • Use a calculator when appropriate.
  • Complete short shopping scenarios.
  • Students working more independently:
  • Calculate totals involving multiple items.
  • Work with dollars and cents.
  • Calculate change from larger amounts.
  • Complete shopping word problems.
  • Use a calculator independently when appropriate.
  • Extensions/Enrichment:
  • Students who demonstrate mastery may complete a budgeting activity in which they receive a specific amount of money and determine which items they can afford without exceeding the budget.
  • Example:
  • "You have $10. You want a notebook for $3, a folder for $2, and a pencil for $1. Can you afford all three? How much money will you have left?"

Classroom Environment & Routines

  • The lesson will follow the established classroom routines and behavioral expectations already used by the cooperating teacher.
  • At the beginning of the lesson, students will be informed of the learning objective and expectations for participation.
  • The teacher will:
  • Provide clear, concise directions.
  • Use a visual schedule/lesson sequence.
  • Review expectations before beginning the classroom-store activity.
  • Provide positive reinforcement for participation and effort.
  • Use proximity and quiet redirection when needed.
  • Allow students appropriate processing time.
  • Provide choices when appropriate.
  • Maintain predictable transitions between activities.
  • Use students' established behavior supports and IEP accommodations.
  • Maintain a respectful and supportive classroom environment.
  • During the simulated shopping activity, students will remain in their assigned areas unless directed to participate in the purchasing activity.

Activating Prior Knowledge

  • The lesson will begin by connecting mathematics to an everyday experience that students may encounter outside of school.
  • The teacher will display several bills and coins and ask:
  • "What is this?"
  • "How much is it worth?"
  • "Which is worth more: $5 or $10?"
  • "If something costs $3 and I have $5, do I have enough money?"
  • The teacher will explain:
  • Teacher Script:
  • "Today we are going to practice something that people do every day, buying things. We are going to use math to figure out how much something costs and how much change we should receive."
  • Students will physically identify or manipulate the money when appropriate.

Materials Needed

  • Play coins and bills
  • Classroom-store items
  • Price tags
  • Shopping worksheets
  • Pencils
  • Calculators
  • Number lines
  • Visual step-by-step procedure
  • Whiteboard/interactive board
  • Teacher observation checklist
  • Exit-ticket problems
  • Individual student materials/accommodations identified in IEPs

Vocabulary

  •  Price: How much something costs.
  •  Cost: The amount of money needed to purchase something.
  •  Total: The amount altogether.
  •  Pay: To give money for something.
  •  Change: The money returned after paying for an item.
  •  Dollar: A unit of money worth 100 cents.
  •  Cents: Units of money smaller than one dollar.
  •  Budget: A plan for how much money can be spent.

Lesson Sequence

  • 0–5 Minutes — Warm-Up/Review
  • Teacher Activities
  • The teacher will display several coins and bills.
  • Teacher Script:
  • "Let's start by reviewing money. What is this?"
  • "How much is this worth?"
  • "Which is worth more: $5 or $10?"
  • "If something costs $2 and I have $5, do I have enough money?"
  • The teacher will use student responses to determine whether additional review is needed.
  • Student Activities
  • Students will:
  • Identify money.
  • State or indicate monetary values.
  • Compare amounts.
  • Respond verbally, by pointing, selecting an answer, or using another established communication method.
  • Formative Assessment
  • The teacher will observe students' ability to identify and compare monetary values.
  • 5–10 Minutes — Introduce Objective and Strategy
  • The teacher will display the student-friendly objective:
  • "I can use money to buy something and figure out my change."
  • The teacher will introduce the five-step strategy:
  • LOOK → ADD → PAY → SUBTRACT → CHECK
  • Teacher Script:
  • "These are our five steps today."
  • "First, we LOOK at the price."
  • "Second, we ADD if we are buying more than one thing."
  • "Third, we decide how much we are going to PAY."
  • "Fourth, we SUBTRACT to find our change."
  • "Last, we CHECK our answer."
  • Students will repeat the sequence or demonstrate it using the visual.
  • 10–18 Minutes — Teacher Modeling
  • The teacher will use play money to model several examples.
  • Example 1
  • Pencil = $1
  • Teacher:
  • "The pencil costs $1. If I pay with $1, how much change will I receive?"
  • Teacher will demonstrate:
  • $1 − $1 = $0
  • Example 2
  • Notebook = $3
  • Teacher:
  • "The notebook costs $3. I am going to pay with $5. How much change will I receive?"
  • Teacher will demonstrate:
  • $5 − $3 = $2
  • The teacher will physically demonstrate the transaction using play money.
  • Example 3
  • Pencil = $1
  • Notebook = $3
  • Teacher:
  • "I want both items. What should I do first?"
  • Students should identify that the prices need to be added.
  • $1 + $3 = $4
  • Teacher:
  • "If I pay with $5, how much change should I receive?"
  • $5 − $4 = $1
  • The teacher will model the complete process.
  • 18–30 Minutes — Guided Practice
  • The teacher will introduce a small classroom store.
  • Example items:
  • Pencil — $1
  • Folder — $2
  • Notebook — $3
  • Water — $1.50
  • School supply — $2.50
  • Students will work through purchasing situations with teacher support.
  • Example:
  • Teacher:
  • "Choose one item."
  • "What is the price?"
  • "How much money are you going to pay?"
  • "How much change should you receive?"
  • Students will use play money and/or calculators according to their instructional level and accommodations.
  • Teacher Questioning/Prompts
  • The teacher will avoid immediately giving students answers and instead use prompts such as:
  • "What does the price tag say?"
  • "What is our first step?"
  • "Do we need to add?"
  • "How much are you paying?"
  • "What operation should we use?"
  • "Does your answer make sense?"
  • "How can you check?"
  • The teacher will provide immediate feedback and reteach when needed.
  • 30–40 Minutes — Individualized Practice
  • Students will complete practice activities matched to their instructional level.
  • Foundational Group
  • Students will:
  • Identify money.
  • Match money to prices.
  • Select appropriate money for one-item purchases.
  • Developing Group
  • Students will:
  • Add two item prices.
  • Determine change from $5 or $10.
  • Use visual supports.
  • More Independent Group
  • Students will:
  • Calculate totals for multiple items.
  • Calculate change.
  • Work with dollars and cents.
  • Complete short shopping word problems.
  • The teacher will circulate, monitor student performance, provide appropriate prompts, and document student progress.
  • 40–45 Minutes —

Closure

  • and Exit Ticket
  • The teacher will bring the lesson to a close by reviewing the five steps.
  • Teacher Script:
  • "Before we finish, who can tell me our five steps?"
  • Students will identify:
  • LOOK → ADD → PAY → SUBTRACT → CHECK
  • The teacher will then provide each student with one individualized exit-ticket problem.
  • Example:
  • A notebook costs $3.
  • You pay with $5.
  • How much change should you receive?
  • Students may answer by:
  • Writing the answer.
  • Using play money.
  • Using a calculator.
  • Pointing to the correct answer.
  • Verbally responding.
  • Demonstrating the transaction.
  • The teacher will use the final response as evidence of student understanding.
  • Preview of Next Lesson
  • The teacher will explain:
  • "Next time, we will use what we learned today to practice staying within a budget and deciding what we can afford to buy."
  • Closure:
  • The lesson will conclude with students reviewing the five-step process:
  • LOOK → ADD → PAY → SUBTRACT → CHECK
  • Students will demonstrate their understanding through an individualized exit ticket.
  • The teacher will use student performance to determine whether the class is ready to progress to budgeting and more complex purchasing situations or whether additional instruction on identifying money, adding prices, or calculating change is needed.
  • Overall Assessment
  • Following the lesson, the student teacher will reflect on:
  • Whether the objective was appropriate for the students' instructional levels.
  • Which students demonstrated mastery.
  • Which students required prompting.
  • Which accommodations were effective.
  • Whether the pacing was appropriate for the 45-minute class period.
  • Whether the classroom-store activity supported student engagement.
  • Which mathematical skills require additional instruction.
  • Whether the lesson should be adjusted before the next instructional session.
  • The student teacher will use the cooperating teacher's feedback and student performance data to determine appropriate changes for future instruction.
