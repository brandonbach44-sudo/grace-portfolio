Emotional Awareness

Emotional Awareness 9_22.docx

09/22, 09/29Social & Emotional SkillsEssential Skills (ESS)

PA Standards

  • PA Career Education and Work Standard 13.3.11.A: Analyze how personal beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors affect decision-making and interactions with others.
  • PA SEL Competency — Self-Awareness: Identify and label emotions; recognize how emotions influence behavior.

Essential Questions

  • How does being aware of our emotions affect the way we respond to situations?
  • How can understanding our emotions help us manage them in healthy ways?

Objectives

  • Students will be able to identify the emotion a person might feel in a given situation.
  • Students will be able to explain how noticing a feeling before reacting (emotional awareness) affects the way a person responds to a situation.
  • Students will be able to identify at least one calming/coping strategy to use when experiencing a strong emotion.
  • Students will be able to describe how a specific emotion (e.g., fear, anger, sadness) can serve a helpful purpose.

Assessment

  • Verbal or nonverbal responses during the 'What Might They Do?' group situation activity
  • Active participation in the guided breathing/calming exercise
  • Completed Emotional Awareness worksheet (ways emotions can help us)
  • Completed 'How Might You Feel' worksheet

Differentiation

  • Visual schedule posted and referenced throughout to support predictability
  • Concrete, literal framing of abstract ideas (e.g., 'Situation → Feeling → What I Do' chain, feelings thermometer/scale)
  • Option to pass or respond nonverbally (pointing, writing) during group discussion
  • Simplified worksheet version with fewer scenarios and sentence starters for the student in the life skills program
  • Extra processing time before being asked to respond aloud
  • Teacher modeling and think-alouds provided before independent worksheet work

Classroom Environment & Routines

  • Visual schedule displayed and referenced at the start of class and during transitions
  • Consistent daily routine: check-in → daily engagement → group activity → direct instruction → independent worksheet → closure
  • Clear verbal and visual cues signal transitions between activities
  • Group discussion norms reinforced: one person talks at a time, 'pass' is always an option
  • Calm, low-pressure tone maintained, especially during emotional-awareness discussion

Activating Prior Knowledge

  • Students enter and complete their daily check-in using the visual schedule posted on the board.
  • Daily engagement activity (Wordle, Contexto, or a set game) to settle in and warm up.
  • Group activity — 'What Might They Do?': A situation is displayed on the board. Say: "I'm going to put a situation on the board. I want you to think, not about what emotion the person feels yet, but about what they might do in that moment. There's no wrong answer, we're just guessing together." Take several guesses per situation.
  • Ask: "If it's the same situation, why might two people react differently?" (Pause for guesses.) Say: "A lot of the time, it comes down to the feeling underneath the action. What someone feels affects what they do, even when they don't realize it's happening." This motivates today's focus on emotional awareness.

Materials Needed

  • Whiteboard/projector with visual schedule and situation prompts
  • Daily engagement activity (Wordle/Contexto/set game)
  • Emotional Awareness worksheet (ways our emotions can help us)
  • "How Might You Feel" worksheet
  • Breathing/calming meditation video
  • Pencils

Vocabulary

  • Emotional awareness — noticing what you are feeling, especially before it turns into an action
  • Trigger — the situation or event that causes a feeling
  • Coping/calming strategy — a tool used to manage a strong feeling
  • React vs. respond — reacting automatically vs. pausing to choose an action

Lesson Sequence

  • Introduce emotional awareness: "That's what we're focusing on today, not just what people do, but the feeling that's driving it. This is called emotional awareness, noticing what you're feeling, and noticing it before it turns into an action you didn't mean to do."
  • Explain why awareness matters: "Feelings happen fast, and sometimes we react before we even realize what we're feeling. But if you pause and notice 'hey, I'm feeling frustrated' or 'I'm feeling nervous,' that little bit of awareness gives you a moment to decide what to do next, instead of just reacting on autopilot." Note that this pause also gives you a chance to use a calming/coping strategy.
  • Introduce the idea that emotions have a purpose: "Big emotions don't always feel good, but even uncomfortable emotions are trying to help us in some way, they're giving us information. Today's worksheet is about figuring out what that information is, and how each emotion can actually work for us instead of against us."
  • Give examples before handing out the Emotional Awareness worksheet: Fear/Nervousness → keeps us safe or prepared ("If you feel nervous before a test, that's your brain telling you 'this matters, get ready.' That nervous feeling can actually push you to study or review your notes."); Anger/Frustration → signals something feels unfair or a boundary was crossed ("If you feel frustrated when a friend keeps interrupting you, that feeling is pointing out that something doesn't feel right, and it might be a signal to speak up or ask for space.")
  • Read through the Emotional Awareness worksheet instructions together and have students answer the question at the bottom.
  • Transition to the calming tool: "We talked about how being aware of how you are feeling can affect your actions: when you notice a feeling before you react, you get a chance to choose your response instead of just reacting automatically. But noticing a feeling is only the first step. Sometimes, especially with big feelings, frustration, anxiety, anger, noticing isn't enough on its own. You also need a way to actually calm that feeling down before you can think clearly and choose your next move. That's where having a tool comes in. One simple tool is a short breathing or calming exercise, something you can use anytime a feeling starts to feel like too much. Let's practice one right now, together, so it's already familiar for whenever you actually need it."
  • Play guided breathing/calming meditation video as a whole group.
  • Introduce independent practice: "Okay, now I want you to practice being aware of your emotions. You have a worksheet called 'How Might You Feel.' I want you to read through these scenarios and write down how you might feel in these situations. You don't have to write this part down, but I want you to think about what you might do to make yourself feel better in these situations or what your emotions in these situations mean."
  • Students work independently on the 'How Might You Feel' worksheet; circulate to support as needed.

Closure

  • Students hand in both worksheets (Emotional Awareness and How Might You Feel).
  • Briefly recap the big idea: noticing a feeling gives you a chance to choose your response, and calming tools help when a feeling gets big.
  • Preview that tomorrow's lesson will continue building on these skills.
  • Students with time remaining work on any missing assignments.
